A free-trade zone project in the Ciskei "Bantustan" in South Africa. In the 1980s, South Africa was divided between territories where land was owned by large white landowners, and Bantustans — semi-autonomous or even nominally independent zones in the east of the country where Black people were concentrated. Ciskei was one of these enclaves, a pseudo-independent nation until 1991.

From 1986 onward, Ciskei's dictator appointed a commission to integrate Ciskei into international trade while making use of the Black sub-proletariat created by apartheid. Living conditions there were poor, Black labour was abundant and cheap, and the territory bordered the sea. The technocrats on this commission, white South Africans, proposed setting up a free-trade zone (the press spoke of "Africa's Hong Kong" and "Africa's Switzerland") in order to attract international investment.

Ciskei became an "Export Processing Zone" (EPZ), a free zone with regulations favourable to international investment (tax exemptions for foreign investors, privatisation of the traditional land tenure system, etc.). European, South African and Asian business circles quickly showed interest, some even opening factories there.

While Ciskei underwent a military coup and was then absorbed into South Africa in 1994, its EPZ disappeared.
